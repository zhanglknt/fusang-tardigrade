## ABSTRACT

Multiple sequence alignment (MSA) scales poorly with dataset size and introduces systematic errors under insertions and deletions (indels), yet alignment-free methods have historically underperformed MSA-based maximum likelihood (ML) approaches. We present Fusang: Tardigrade Edition, an alignment-free phylogenetics tool that infers trees directly from unaligned input — with automatic parameter selection, a multi-k distance ensemble that removes manual k choice, and a random-forest boundary classifier that maps its domain of applicability. Its inference engine, k-mer frequency vector cosine distances (spaced and contiguous patterns, systematically evaluated here), is numerically, but not statistically, better than FastTree2 on simulated indel-rich data at n=200 (indel rate=0.02: nRF=0.199±0.030 vs 0.205±0.030, 130 seeds, Wilcoxon p=0.0507 — borderline at α=0.05, not significant after correction, and reversed at n=1000 indel, where FastTree2 is significantly better), while MSA methods retain a significant advantage on clean data at every scale tested. The multi-k ensemble reduces topological error by 8.7% relative to single-k under indel-rich simulation (nRF=0.137±0.023 vs 0.150±0.026, 30 seeds, p=0.0018), reaching to within ~11% of MAFFT+FastTree2 (0.124±0.026) without alignment but not matching it (p=0.0024 in favour of MSA+ML). On a real indel-rich mitochondrial benchmark (25 genomes, 9.1% gaps), the ensemble reaches the community-benchmark ceiling — nRF=0.045, matched by MSA+ML and all best published alignment-free methods on this single small dataset. MinHash (Mash) is far less accurate than k-mer cosine on gene-length data in both conditions (clean 0.349 vs 0.236; indel 0.403 vs 0.193; 30 seeds, Fusang better in 30/30 seeds, d≈3.1 and 2.6), and the two respond to indels in opposite directions — Mash degrades modestly (1.16×) while k-mer cosine improves (0.83×). At gene length, contiguous k=7 (0.194) and the multi-k ensemble (0.194) are both significantly more accurate than Fusang's default spaced+NNI pipeline (0.199, p=0.0039 and 0.0077), which we therefore present as a robust rather than an optimal default. Cross-domain validation on 11 SwissTree protein families confirms k-mer frequency methods outperform context-matching by 2.2–2.4× (p=0.0010–0.0020). The boundary classifier separates homogeneous from structured datasets (150/150 simulated scenarios). Fusang infers trees for 10,000 taxa in ~70 s on a 4-core workstation, with pre-compiled binaries for Windows and Linux.

## INTRODUCTION

Phylogenetic inference is foundational to evolutionary biology, from tracing viral outbreaks [1] to reconstructing the tree of life [2]. The standard workflow — multiple sequence alignment (MSA) followed by maximum likelihood (ML) or Bayesian tree search — faces two intractable challenges. First, MSA computation scales as O(n²L²), making it prohibitive for datasets exceeding thousands of taxa. Second, alignment quality degrades systematically when sequences contain insertions and deletions (indels). Alignment algorithms must place gaps heuristically, and each gap placement introduces potential error that propagates through phylogenetic inference [3,4,5]. Yet indel-rich evolution is the biological norm — from viral quasispecies to orthologous gene families — and the impact of indel-induced alignment errors remains understudied in method benchmarking.

### The Fusang lineage: from deep learning to k-mer features

We previously introduced Fusang v1 [6], a deep learning-based phylogenetic inference tool that bypasses MSA through learned feature representations. While Fusang v1 demonstrated that alignment-free methods could produce biologically meaningful trees, it was limited to 4–40 taxa and required pre-trained neural network models. In this work, we present Fusang: Tardigrade Edition, a fundamentally re-architected framework that replaces deep learning with spaced k-mer feature extraction — achieving comparable accuracy, unlimited taxon scalability, and orders-of-magnitude speed improvements without requiring GPU acceleration or model training.

### K-mer frequency vectors in phylogenetics: status and opportunities

K-mer frequency vectors have been applied to multiple sequence analysis tasks including protein classification [7], metagenomic binning [8], and genome assembly. In phylogenetic inference, prior alignment-free work (reviewed in [9,10]) includes hierarchical phylogenomic inference [11], kmacs [12] (gapped k-mismatch substring matching), spaced-word frequency methods [13], and the Alfpy toolkit with the AFproject benchmark [14], which provides standardized implementations of multiple alignment-free methods including gapped k-mer variants. Despite these foundations, **k-mer frequency vectors (spaced and contiguous) have not been systematically evaluated for phylogenetic inference under realistic evolutionary conditions (indel-rich sequences).** The contributions of this work are therefore twofold: (i) a systematic evaluation of **k-mer frequency vector cosine distances** — comparing both spaced and contiguous k-mer patterns across multiple distance metrics — for phylogenetic inference under indel-rich conditions; and (ii) the delivery of these findings as a ready-to-use open-source tool (Fusang: Tardigrade Edition) with automatic parameter selection, a multi-k ensemble requiring no manual k choice, and an empirically mapped domain of applicability. We find that: (1) k-mer frequency vectors with cosine distance provide effective phylogenetic signal under indels; (2) spaced k-mers are hypothesized to be more robust at high indel rates, though the practical advantage over contiguous k-mers is modest at the tested indel rate (0.02); and (3) MinHash-based methods are far less accurate than k-mer cosine at gene length and, under indels, move in the opposite direction to it (Mash degrades 1.16× while k-mer cosine improves 0.83×), so the earlier claim that both degrade severely (~1.9×) is withdrawn. The multi-k ensemble, which fuses distance matrices across multiple k-mer resolutions, provides robust accuracy without requiring manual parameter selection, and represents a practical contribution alongside the representation evaluation.

### Contributions of this work

We systematically evaluate spaced k-mer features for phylogenetic tree inference across datasets spanning n=20 to 10,000 taxa. We make the following contributions:

1. **Vector-based phylogenetic inference under indels.** Fusang's multi-k ensemble NJ reduces topological error by 8.7% relative to the single-k configuration (TRUE-relative nRF=0.137±0.023 vs 0.150±0.026, 30 seeds, Wilcoxon p=0.0018, paired d=0.67, ensemble lower in 19/30 seeds). On the same 30-seed benchmark, MAFFT+FastTree2 is significantly more accurate than the ensemble (0.124±0.026, paired Wilcoxon p=0.0024, d=0.64, MSA+ML lower in 22/30), so we present the ensemble as reaching to within ~11% relative error of MSA+ML without computing an alignment — not as matching or exceeding it. Earlier versions of this manuscript claimed the opposite ordering (ensemble better, p=0.024) and a 21.5% fusion gain; both came from data produced by a hand-written coalescent simulator that we now regard as defective, and both are withdrawn (see Results). The multi-k ensemble provides robust accuracy without manual k selection.

2. **Characterization of indel robustness and comparison with MinHash approaches.** Fusang is numerically more accurate than FastTree2 under indels at n=200 (TRUE-relative nRF=0.199±0.030 vs FastTree2 nRF=0.205±0.030, paired Wilcoxon p=0.0507, 130 seeds; borderline at α=0.05, not significant after Bonferroni correction across the six ground-truth datasets, and reversed at n=1000 indel, where FastTree2 is significantly better). MinHash (Mash) is far less accurate than k-mer cosine in absolute terms (clean 0.349 vs 0.236; indel 0.403 vs 0.193; 30 seeds, Fusang better in 30/30 seeds in both conditions, paired d=3.12 and 2.61), and the two respond to indels in opposite directions: Mash degrades modestly but significantly (1.16×, p=3.0×10⁻³) while k-mer cosine improves (0.83×, p=6.2×10⁻⁶); the earlier claim that both degrade ~1.9× was an artefact of a defective simulator and is withdrawn. On the same 130-seed benchmark, spaced k=5 is significantly more accurate than contiguous k=5 under indels (0.2003 vs 0.2078, p=5.54×10⁻⁴), whereas BME-NNI refinement adds no significant gain (p=0.34); contiguous k=7 (0.1938) and the multi-k ensemble (0.1940) are both significantly more accurate than the default pipeline (0.1990, p=3.9×10⁻³ and 7.7×10⁻³), which we present as robust rather than optimal at gene length. Cross-domain validation on protein families confirms k-mer frequency methods outperform context-matching by 2.2–2.4× (p=0.0010–0.0020).

3. **Engineering contributions: DCM degradation analysis, adaptive pipeline, and multi-k ensemble.** We identify EPA grafting as the primary bottleneck in divide-and-conquer pipelines and implement an adaptive strategy that automatically selects optimal pipeline based on dataset size. The multi-k ensemble fuses distance matrices across k-mer resolutions.

4. **Open-source implementation with automated parameter selection and boundary classifier.** Fusang includes a random forest classifier for dataset suitability assessment (100% accuracy on 150 simulated scenarios, 95% CI [0.975, 1.0]) and provides pre-compiled binaries for Windows/Linux.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

### Spaced k-mer feature extraction

For a DNA sequence S of length L, a spaced k-mer of weight k is defined by a binary pattern over a window of length k + s (s = number of skipped positions), in which k positions are sampled (1) and s are skipped (0). Two gap styles are implemented (`kmer_distance.make_gap_pattern`): `gap1` interleaves one skipped position after every two sampled positions, and `gap2` (the default) splits the sampled positions into two contiguous blocks separated by two skipped positions in the middle. Concretely, at the default k=5 the `gap2` pattern is 1110011 (5 sampled positions spanning 7 nucleotides, positions 0,1,2,5,6), and at k=4 the `gap1` pattern is 11011 (4 sampled positions spanning 5 nucleotides, positions 0,1,3,4); at k=4 `gap2` gives 110011 (positions 0,1,4,5). Contiguous k-mers are the special case of no skipped positions. (An earlier description of this scheme stated that k=5,g=2 yields 1001001001001 and k=4,g=1 yields 1010101; that was incorrect and did not match the implementation, which is as stated here. All results in this paper were produced by the implementation, so only the description was affected.) For the 20-letter protein alphabet the SwissTree benchmark (Table 10) uses shorter patterns — `1011` (3 sampled positions spanning 4 residues) and `11011` (4 sampled positions spanning 5 residues) — to keep the frequency-vector dimension tractable.

For a sequence S, the normalized frequency vector F(S) ∈ [0,1]^(4^w) — where w is the number of sampled positions in the pattern (w=k for contiguous k-mers) — counts occurrences of each observed k-mer pattern, normalized to unit L1-norm. **Strand handling.** The implementation counts forward-strand k-mers only; it does not apply reverse-complement canonicalisation (i.e. it does not replace each k-mer by the lexicographically smaller of itself and its reverse complement). An earlier version of this Methods section claimed such canonicalisation "ensures strand-invariance"; that statement did not describe the code and is withdrawn. All benchmarks reported here use sequences given on a single consistent strand — simulated data are generated 5'→3' from the root, and the real datasets are provided in a single orientation — so strand-invariance is not exercised by any result in this paper. For inputs mixing both strands (e.g. unassembled reads in arbitrary orientation), canonicalisation or explicit strand correction would be required, and we note this as a limitation rather than as an evaluated property.

### K-mer distance computation

Pairwise distances between sequences A and B are computed using two complementary metrics:

**Cosine distance** (used in the simplified pipeline):
D_cos(A,B) = 1 − cos(F(A), F(B))

Cosine distance is preferred for the simplified pipeline because it directly models frequency vector direction, which better preserves phylogenetic signal when no downstream transformations (DCM, EPA) are applied. **Comparison with Jensen-Shannon divergence (JSD):** JSD, used in the DCM pipeline's TF-IDF weighting step, provides a normalized distance metric suitable for high-dimensional sparse frequency vectors. However, in a benchmark on INDELible-simulated data (n=200, indel rate=0.02, 30 seeds, seeds 100–129) rescored with the standard root-independent unrooted RF, cosine distance achieved mean nRF=0.196 ± 0.026 vs square-root JSD mean nRF=0.205 ± 0.029 (paired Wilcoxon p=0.011 on integer RF counts, paired Cohen's d=0.38; cosine better in 22/30 seeds, square-root JSD better in 7, 1 tie); the earlier figures (0.129 ± 0.027 vs 0.132 ± 0.025, p=0.147) were produced by the superseded root-dependent nRF implementation and are withdrawn. Rescoring script and per-seed values: `repro_ablations/cosine_vs_jsd_nrfstandard.py`, `repro_ablations/cosine_vs_jsd_n200_indel_30seeds_nrfstandard.json`. An independent rerun of the same configuration in Table 2 gives a cosine baseline of 0.193; the 0.003 difference stems from tie-breaking among near-equal distances in the NJ joins (the two runs differ by at most one split on any seed, 11 of 30 seeds differing at all), and does not change any conclusion. This comparison informed the heuristic choice of cosine distance for the simplified pipeline; it is a single 30-seed exploratory comparison on simulated data and is not presented as a validated general advantage. The advantage likely arises because cosine distance emphasizes the direction of frequency vectors (relative frequencies) over their magnitude, which is more robust to the varying sequence lengths induced by indels. JSD, while theoretically well-motivated as a symmetric KL-divergence variant, is more sensitive to magnitude differences in high-dimensional sparse vectors. Both metrics achieve lower absolute nRF than MinHash Jaccard under indels (see Results).

**Jensen-Shannon divergence** (used in the DCM pipeline):
D_JSD(A,B) = JSD(F(A), F(B)) = ½ D_KL(P||M) + ½ D_KL(Q||M)

where M = ½(P+Q) and D_KL is the Kullback-Leibler divergence. JSD is symmetric and bounded in [0, ln(2)], providing normalized distances suitable for TF-IDF weighting in DCM.

### Adaptive pipeline architecture

Fusang implements two distinct tree-building strategies selected automatically based on dataset size:

#### Simplified pipeline (n ≤ 1000, default)

For small-to-medium datasets, Fusang bypasses divide-and-conquer entirely:
1. Extract spaced k-mer frequency vectors for all sequences
2. Compute pairwise cosine distances (O(n² × 4^k))
3. Build a tree via Neighbor-Joining (NJ, BioPython) or FastME BIONJ+BNNI (BIONJ: bioNJ, a modified NJ algorithm with improved theoretical properties; BNNI: Bayesian Nearest Neighbor Interchange, a tree topology search heuristic)
4. When the tree was built by NJ and n ≥ 100, apply BME-scored NNI refinement (`nni_refine`, at most 100 accepted moves); this step is skipped for FastME trees, which are already BME-optimal
5. Output Newick tree
6. (Optional) Compute bootstrap support values via multinomial resampling of k-mer profiles (see Supplementary Note S5)

Unless explicitly noted as "FastME," all simplified-pipeline **accuracy** benchmarks in this work use the NJ builder with the BME-scored NNI refinement step (auto mode; applied for n ≥ 100). The SwissTree benchmark and the alignment-free competitor comparison (see Results) likewise use NJ, for consistency with the AFproject community standard, which requires identical tree builders across methods. Scalability (wall-clock) benchmarks use FastME for its O(n²) speed advantage (Table 11).

This pipeline was discovered through systematic ablation experiments (see Results). On the 130-seed INDELible clean benchmark (n=200), it attains nRF=0.144 ± 0.025 against FastTree2's 0.146 ± 0.025 (paired Wilcoxon p=0.242, not significant; Table 1). The simplified pipeline eliminates the DCM-related degradation observed in the original architecture.

#### Divide-and-conquer pipeline (n > 500)

For large datasets, Fusang employs the Disk-Covering Method (DCM [3,15]):
1. **Clustering**: Pairwise k-mer distances → hierarchical clustering (scipy, average linkage) into groups of ≤200 taxa with 10–20% overlap
2. **Backbone tree**: Representative centroid sequences → FastME NJ tree
3. **Subtree inference**: Within-cluster trees via FastME BIONJ+BNNI
4. **Grafting**: Subtrees attached to backbone via Evolutionary Placement Algorithm (EPA [16])

The adaptive pipeline selects between simplified and DCM+EPA modes based on dataset size, controlled by the `SIMPLE_THRESHOLD=500` parameter. For n≤500, Fusang uses the simplified pipeline (k-mer→cosine→NJ plus BME-scored NNI refinement for n≥100) by default, since DCM adds no accuracy benefit at small-to-medium scales. For n>500, Fusang switches to DCM+EPA for scalability. An earlier configuration with the threshold set to 2,000 forced the simplified pipeline at n=1000 and was reverted after causing an accuracy regression; the n=1000 accuracy figures behind that choice were produced by a superseded nRF implementation and are therefore omitted here (see Results).

### FastME integration

FastME v2.1.6.4 [17] is the default tree builder. FastME implements the Minimum Evolution principle with bioNJ [18] as the initial topology and BNNI (Branch and Bound Nearest Neighbor Interchange) as the optimization step. The implementation discovers FastME via a priority cascade:
1. Bundled Windows-native binary (`fastme_bin/fastme.exe`)
2. WSL-installed binary (`/usr/local/bin/fastme`)
3. Project-bundled Linux binary (`fastme_bin/fastme_linux`)

The Windows-native binary (PE32+ x86-64, 725 KB) eliminates the WSL dependency, enabling single-command operation on native Windows with zero cross-system overhead.

Benchmark timings for the tree-building stage alone: at n=200, NJ 1.3 s → FastME 0.4 s (3.5× speedup); at n=500, 13.2 s → 0.9 s (15.1×); at n=1000, 104.3 s → 3.6 s (28.8×).

### Adaptive parameter selection

Fusang automatically selects optimal k and gap parameters based on the number of input taxa n:
- n ≤ 100: k=4,gap1 (emphasizes local conservation for shallow divergence)
- n > 100: k=5,gap2 (captures intermediate-range correlations for moderate divergence)

This adaptive strategy was derived from stability benchmarking across n=20/50/100/200/500 (see Results). Manual override is available via command-line flags.

### Benchmark design

#### Simulated data (without indels)

All simulated benchmark datasets were generated with INDELible v1.03 [19]. Substitutions followed a general time-reversible (GTR) model with exchangeabilities a=0.2, b=0.4, c=0.6, d=0.8, e=1.2 (f=1, i.e. the standard INDELible GTR example rates) and equal base frequencies; among-site rate heterogeneity was modelled with a discrete gamma distribution (shape α=1.0, 4 rate categories). Trees were drawn from a birth–death prior (speciation rate λ=1.0, extinction rate μ=0.5) conditioned on n extant tips; extinct lineages were pruned, tips were extended to the sampling time by an exponential waiting time, and branch lengths were rescaled so that the mean branch length corresponds to 0.005 expected substitutions per site — the divergence level of the pilot simulations (substitution rate 0.05 subs/site per unit branch length × branch length 0.1). Root sequence length L=500 bp. Dataset sizes: n=20, 50, 100, 200, 500, 1000, 10000.

**Sequence length choice (L=500 bp):** This length was selected to represent typical protein-coding gene sequences (e.g., bacterial 16S rRNA ~1,500 bp; mitochondrial COI ~650 bp; bacterial housekeeping genes ~500–800 bp). While longer sequences (L>1000 bp) would provide more phylogenetic signal, L=500 bp represents a challenging real-world scenario where alignment errors are non-negligible. We note that Fusang's relative accuracy advantage over MSA methods grows with indel rate but may diminish for longer sequences where alignment quality improves. Extending benchmarks to L=1000–2000 bp represents important future work.

#### Simulated data (with indels)

Insertions and deletions occurred alongside substitutions at equal rates, with lengths drawn from a geometric distribution of mean 3 bp. In INDELible this is specified as a negative binomial `NB q=2/3, r=1`, whose mean is 1 + r·q/(1−q) = 3 bp; we verified the realised indel-length distribution against theory in a two-taxon control experiment (observed SD 3.8 bp vs theoretical 3.9 bp). Indel rates are reported throughout as the absolute per-site indel rate (0.005, 0.01, 0.02, 0.05), i.e. ρ = 0.1, 0.2, 0.4 and 1.0 relative to the substitution rate. Because INDELible's `[indelrate]` parameter sets the insertion rate and the deletion rate *separately*, the control-file value was set to ρ/2 so that the total indel event rate matches the intended ρ. Multi-seed benchmarks used 130 independent seeds (birth–death tree seed and INDELible random seed both derived from the same integer) for statistical power.

**Simulation reproducibility.** The tree generator (`gen_indelible_sim.py`; pure Python standard library, deterministic given a seed), the INDELible control files it emits, and the benchmark driver (`run_bench_indelible.py`) are included in the reproduction package, together with a pinned Conda environment specification (`environment.yml`: Python 3.11, MAFFT 7.526, FastTree 2.2.0, IQ-TREE 2.4.0). Every simulated dataset — FASTA, true tree, alignment, and both inferred trees — is regenerable from its seed with the exact software versions recorded here. Two toolchain consistency checks were performed: (i) regenerating the n=200/seed=100 dataset reproduced the committed FASTA byte-for-byte; and (ii) re-running the full n=200 indel benchmark under the pinned environment reproduced the reported Fusang mean within 0.0004 nRF and the FastTree2 trees bit-for-bit (130/130), with per-seed Fusang differences confined to ±1–2 bipartitions arising from BLAS-dependent floating-point tie-breaking in NJ across library versions (within-environment runs are deterministic: three repeats of the same seed produced bit-identical trees). Fusang per-seed values should therefore be reproduced under the pinned environment; summary statistics are robust to these platform effects. The n=500/n=1000 benchmarks additionally used MAFFT 7.525 with the identical FastTree 2.2.0 binary (toolchain versions are recorded per benchmark in the reproduction package).

#### Statistical framework

For multi-seed benchmarks, we report:
- Mean and standard deviation of nRF across seeds
- Wilcoxon signed-rank test p-values (paired per-seed comparison). Tests are run on **integer RF counts**, not on normalised nRF values: feeding independently rounded floating-point nRF values into the test introduces ~10⁻⁶ numerical noise that breaks rank ties and can change the resulting p-value. Zero differences are handled with the zero-split convention (`zero_method='zsplit'`, zeros split evenly between the positive and negative ranks); this convention is used for **every** Wilcoxon signed-rank test reported in this manuscript, so no comparison is reported under whichever convention happens to favour it
- Cohen's d effect size with 95% bootstrap confidence intervals
- Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons across datasets (6 ground truth datasets tested — n=200 clean, n=200 indel, n=500 clean, n=500 indel, n=1000 clean, n=1000 indel; adjusted α = 0.05/6 ≈ 0.008)
- Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction as a less conservative alternative. The 5 ground truth dataset comparisons constitute the pre-specified confirmatory family; all other reported p-values (e.g., SwissTree, alignment-free competitor, and pipeline-level comparisons) are secondary and should be interpreted as exploratory

All statistical analyses were performed in Python using scipy.stats. We note that statistical power varies across dataset sizes: the 130-seed benchmark (n=200, indel) provides adequate power (≥80%) to detect a medium effect size (Cohen's d=0.5) at α=0.05 (two-sided paired test), while 30-seed benchmarks provide moderate power (~75% for d=0.5) and should be interpreted cautiously for non-significant results.

**Outlier handling**: For all multi-seed benchmarks, we exclude seeds where nRF > 0.3 for either method. This threshold identifies trees where over 30% of all possible bipartitions are incorrect — a point at which the tree carries negligible topological information (for n=200, nRF=0.3 corresponds to ~118 bipartition mismatches, approaching the stochastic expectation of a random tree). In our data, these outliers invariably correspond to MAFFT alignment failures (empty output, rc=0) under heavy indels and are excluded to report tree quality rather than alignment robustness. Crucially, this threshold is applied symmetrically to both Fusang and comparator methods, and is pre-specified before any multi-seed experiment. In the 130-seed INDELible benchmark (n=200, indel rate=0.02, seeds 100–229) all 130 seeds produced valid trees and none exceeded the nRF>0.3 threshold, so no exclusion was applied to the reported statistics; the rule is retained for the remaining conditions, where it does remove occasional MAFFT failure seeds. Complete raw data are provided in Supplementary Table S4.

#### Accuracy metric

Normalized Robinson-Foulds distance (nRF, root-independent / unrooted):
nRF = RF / (2(n − 3)) = (|S₁ \ S₂| + |S₂ \ S₁|) / (2(n − 3))

where S₁ and S₂ are the sets of **non-trivial splits (bipartitions)** of the two trees on their shared leaf set of size n. Each split is canonicalised by keeping the side that does **not** contain the lexicographically smallest leaf, so the measure is independent of rooting position. For a fully resolved unrooted binary tree the number of non-trivial splits is exactly n − 3, making 2(n − 3) the maximum attainable RF between two fully resolved trees; nRF=0 is a perfect match and nRF=1 complete disagreement. Partially resolved trees (e.g. trees containing polytomies or zero-length branches that collapse to multifurcations) contribute fewer than n − 3 splits, which correspondingly increases their RF against a fully resolved reference — we report this because FastTree2 output occasionally contains collapsed branches.

Computation: `nrf_standard.py` in the reproduction package. We verified the implementation against DendroPy 5.0.8 (`treecompare.symmetric_difference` with `rooting='force-unrooted'`) on five independent tree pairs spanning both conditions and three dataset scales, obtaining bit-identical values (e.g. 0.274112, 0.220812, 0.228426, 0.213198, 0.221328). An earlier in-house metric (`calc_nrf_simple.py`), which retained only the smaller child clade of each internal node, captured only 64 of the 197 non-trivial splits of an n=200 reference tree and systematically distorted comparisons in Fusang's favour; **all nRF values reported here use the root-independent definition above**, and every value from the earlier metric has been recomputed. A concrete illustration of the size and direction of that distortion: on clean n=200 data (seed 42, `test_n200.fasta`) the simplified pipeline's NJ tree is itself identical to the simulated true tree (RF=0, nRF=0.000), yet the earlier metric reported nRF=0.005 for it — that is, the superseded implementation invented error that the root-independent metric shows was never there. Errors of this kind, applied per tree and not uniformly across methods, are why the earlier comparisons in this manuscript were biased in Fusang's favour and why every affected value has been recomputed.

#### Comparison methods

- **Fusang: Tardigrade Edition** (this work): spaced k-mers (k=5,gap2), simplified or DCM pipeline, FastME BIONJ+BNNI
- **Fusang v1** [6]: deep learning-based, 4–40 taxa limit
- **FastTree2 v2.2.0** [20]: GTR+CAT approximation, MAFFT alignment [21]
- **RAxML-NG** [22] (version not captured in the run logs; the in-repo benchmark reports carry an evidently erroneous "v2.0.1" label, so no version is asserted): GTR+Γ, 10 parsimony + 10 random starting trees, MAFFT alignment
- **IQ-TREE2 v2.4.0** [23]: GTR model (fixed via `-m GTR`, not auto-selected), MAFFT alignment. IQ-TREE2 was evaluated on n=200 indel data (130 seeds) but failed to complete within practical time limits at larger scales (n=1000: 10/10 seeds timed out at 24h); on the n=200 subset, IQ-TREE2 produces trees 1.06× worse than Fusang's k-mer NJ (standard root-independent unrooted nRF=0.210 ± 0.031 vs 0.199 ± 0.030, paired Wilcoxon p=7.95×10⁻⁵, paired Cohen's d=0.385; Fusang better in 77/130 seeds, IQ-TREE2 better in 46, 7 ties). An earlier version of this manuscript reported 1.9× (0.231 vs 0.122); that figure divided an IQ-TREE2 value produced by one nRF implementation by a Fusang value produced by a different one, and is withdrawn — under either implementation applied consistently, Fusang is 0.199–0.201 and the gap is ≈1.06×. An independent replicate of the IQ-TREE2 runs (130/130 seeds, trees differing topologically on 128/130 seeds due to search stochasticity) gives 0.211 ± 0.031, 1.06×, p=5.2×10⁻⁵, d=0.397, confirming the conclusion is not run-specific. The residual gap likely reflects indel-induced alignment errors propagating to even fixed-model ML inference, illustrating a general limitation of alignment-dependent methods under high indel rates. Full data available in Supplementary Note S10.
- **Mash v2.3** [24]: contiguous k-mers (k=21), MinHash Jaccard, NJ
- **Mash + FastME**: Mimics Fusang pipeline with contiguous k-mers (Mash distance + FastME), serving as a clean ablation control isolating spaced k-mers from pipeline architecture
- **andi-approx** (tested): Python approximation of suffix array-based anchor distance [25]. Tested on SwissTree protein gene families; andi is designed for whole-genome comparisons and is less accurate than k-mer frequency methods on gene-length sequences. See Supplementary Note S4.
- **Co-phylog** [26]: k-mer frequency + covariance matrix eigenvalues. Tested on both DNA and protein benchmarks (see Results).
- **kmacs** [12]: k-mismatch average common substring distance. Tested on the DNA indel benchmark (130 seeds) with a k-parameter scan (k∈{3,5,10}; best configuration k=3, see Results). Compiled from the original source distribution and executed on WSL2 Ubuntu-24.04.

#### Hardware

All benchmarks: Intel Xeon E-2124 (4 cores/4 threads, 3.3 GHz), 32 GB RAM, Windows 10. Fusang runs natively; RAxML-NG/MAFFT executed under WSL2 (Ubuntu 24.04).

### Real data validation: 16S rRNA type strains

We downloaded 74 representative 16S rRNA sequences spanning 13 phyla (NCBI GenBank, Supplementary Table S1) and ran Fusang (k=5,gap2, simplified pipeline) without alignment. The true composition of the analysed set, established by re-annotating every downloaded accession against NCBI Taxonomy, is 73 sequences across 13 phyla (7 represented by ≥2 taxa), 21 classes (9 with ≥2 taxa) and 40 orders (14 with ≥2 taxa), including 5 archaeal sequences (4 Methanobacteriota, 1 Thermoproteota); one intended taxon (*Sulfolobus solfataricus*) failed to download, giving 73 analysed sequences of which 71 carry usable taxonomy. Since no gold-standard reference tree exists, we evaluated topological quality by comparing tree-based pairwise distances against NCBI taxonomic classifications using permutation tests, and by testing monophyly recovery of taxonomic groups. We note that the accession→organism mapping originally shipped with this dataset was erroneous at the genus/species level for all 73 accessions; all analyses reported here therefore use the corrected NCBI Taxonomy annotation, and the incident is disclosed with the remediation steps in Supplementary Note S17.

### Real genome-scale validation: AFproject community datasets

To test the indel-robustness claims on real data beyond simulations, we benchmarked on two real whole-genome datasets from the AFproject community benchmark [14], each with a published trusted reference tree: (1) Fish mtDNA — 25 fish mitochondrial genomes (~17 kb each, spanning multiple orders; reference tree from Fischer et al. 2013); (2) E. coli/Shigella — 27 genomes (~4.5–5.5 Mb; reference tree from Skippington & Ragan 2011). Sequences were downloaded from NCBI (accessions in Supplementary Table S16). For the fish dataset we additionally computed the MAFFT alignment (n=25, 18,284 columns) to quantify indel content: 9.1% of alignment cells are gaps. For the SwissTree protein benchmark we quantified gap content analogously: median 50% (range 11–81%) across the 11 families, confirming that this benchmark is strongly indel-rich. All methods built trees via Neighbor-Joining (BioPython) on unaligned input, scored with ETE3 Tree.compare (unrooted nRF, the AFproject scoring standard); kmacs and Mash were run at both our working configurations and the exact configurations published in the AFproject benchmark, providing an external cross-validation of the benchmark harness (data, NJ, and ETE3 scoring) rather than of Fusang itself. Whole-genome MSA was not computed for the 27-genome E. coli/Shigella dataset (alignment of 27 × 4.6 Mb is impractical — precisely the regime where alignment-free methods are necessary); for the fish dataset, MAFFT+FastTree2 served as the MSA+ML comparator. The ETE3 unrooted nRF used here (informative splits only, max_rf = 2(n−3)) is numerically equivalent to the standard root-independent measure now used throughout Tables 1–11 and validated against DendroPy (see Accuracy metric above). We therefore no longer treat the real-genome figures as belonging to a separate scale; an earlier note flagging them as non-comparable applied to the superseded `calc_nrf_simple.py` implementation and has been removed. The 16S rRNA benchmark (Table 9) already used an equivalent canonical split-set implementation and its values are unchanged. The SwissTree (Table 10) and BAliBASE (Supplementary Table S7) benchmarks did not: both were rescored with the standard metric from re-generated trees (verified to reproduce the original values bit-for-bit under the old implementation, so the changes are attributable to the metric alone and not to pipeline drift), and the resulting changes to their conclusions are stated in Results and Discussion.

### Cross-platform compatibility and deployment

Fusang is implemented in Python 3.10+ with a modular architecture that supports both command-line and script-based workflows. Pre-compiled FastME binaries are provided for Windows (x86-64) and Linux (x86-64), ensuring consistent numerical results across platforms. The simplified pipeline (n ≤ 1000) runs synchronously; larger datasets use the DCM strategy with configurable parallelism (default: 4 workers). A containerized Docker image (python:3.10-slim base) is provided for reproducible deployment.

## RESULTS

### A simplified pipeline achieves state-of-the-art accuracy

Systematic ablation of the Fusang pipeline revealed a critical finding: the divide-and-conquer (DCM) strategy, while essential for scalability, requires careful tuning to avoid accuracy loss at small-to-medium scales. On n=200 indel simulated data (indel rate=0.02, seed=42), step-wise tracing from the simplified pipeline to the full DCM+EPA pipeline isolates the error source (Figure 3, Supplementary Figure S3, Supplementary Table S3; raw trace `repro_ablations/ablation_dcm_seed42_indel_nrfstandard.json`, scored with the standard root-independent unrooted RF):

- Simplified pipeline (k-mer→cosine→NJ): **nRF=0.081**
- Swap in the FastME tree builder: nRF=0.081
- Swap in the `distances.py` distance-matrix implementation: nRF=0.081
- Add TF-IDF weighting: nRF=0.137
- Full DCM with EPA grafting (max_group_size=10): nRF=0.523

For calibration, FastTree2 on the MAFFT alignment of the same data achieves nRF=0.096, and across eleven DCM configurations on this seed (max_group_size=10–150, NJ subtrees) the full-DCM nRF ranged 0.096–0.523 (mean 0.233 ± 0.126). **Provenance**: the earlier trace for this chain was stored in `diag_dcm_loss_n200_s42.json`, which — despite the text above naming indel rate=0.02 — was computed on clean `test_n200.fasta`, and whose stored stage values do not match any number previously printed in this manuscript; the chain reported here was therefore recomputed with the standard root-independent unrooted RF on the dataset the text names (n=200, indel rate=0.02, seed 42; `repro_ablations/ablation_dcm_seed42_indel_nrfstandard.py`). Earlier text specified only "full DCM with EPA grafting" without a `max_group_size`; we now report the `max_group_size=10` setting, whose value under the old scorer (0.2487 on clean data) is the closest to the previously printed 0.251. This choice does not drive the conclusion: all eleven settings (10–150) exceed the simplified pipeline's 0.081, giving 0.096–0.523 (mean 0.233 ± 0.126). The first four steps confirm that the clustering and tree-building logic is sound; the large error appears only at the final step, isolating EPA (Evolutionary Placement Algorithm [16]) grafting as the primary error source. EPA, designed for placing single short reads onto a reference tree, introduces topological errors when grafting entire subtrees with internal structure. An earlier informal trace on a different dataset instance (seed=100) reported a larger step-5 error (nRF=0.388); we could not reproduce that value on the canonical seed-42 benchmark and therefore report the reproducible value here. Multi-seed validation confirms the directional advantage at smaller magnitude (see Section "130-seed benchmark confirms directional advantage").

Based on this finding, we implemented an adaptive pipeline: for n≤500, Fusang uses the simplified pipeline (k-mer→cosine→NJ plus BME-scored NNI refinement for n≥100); for n>500, Fusang switches to DCM+EPA, which provides essential scalability and a small accuracy advantage at scale (n=1000: DCM nRF=0.083 ± 0.005, 9 seeds 401–409, vs simplified nRF=0.102 on seed 401 — obtained under the superseded nRF implementation; the seed 401–409 true trees are no longer in the working tree, so this comparison could not be recomputed with the standard unrooted RF, and it is used here only to document scalability, not as an accuracy result). The `SIMPLE_THRESHOLD=500` parameter controls this transition.

**Note on simplified pipeline accuracy**: The simplified pipeline is competitive with, but does not systematically exceed, MSA-based methods on clean data. On 130 INDELible seeds at n=200 clean (Table 1), Fusang attains nRF=0.144 ± 0.025 vs FastTree2 0.146 ± 0.025 (paired Wilcoxon p=0.242, not significant; Fusang better in 68/130 seeds). The key advantage of Fusang emerges under indel-rich conditions (see below).

### Spaced k-mers close the accuracy gap on clean data

On clean substitution-only data, Fusang's accuracy varies by dataset size (Table 1). On n=200 indel-rich data (indel rate=0.02), Fusang is numerically more accurate than FastTree2, but the difference is small and not statistically significant at α=0.05 (nRF: Fusang 0.199 ± 0.030 vs FastTree2 0.205 ± 0.030, 130 seeds, paired Wilcoxon p=0.0507, paired d=−0.20, Fusang better in 71/130 seeds, FastTree2 better in 53, 6 ties); it does not survive Bonferroni correction across the six ground-truth datasets. **Reporting convention for this comparison.** The test is run on integer RF counts rather than on normalised nRF values (130 seeds, 6 ties). Under the classical "discard zero-differences" convention the p-value is 0.0497; under the zero-split convention (`zero_method='zsplit'`), which is applied uniformly to every Wilcoxon signed-rank test in this manuscript, it is p=0.0507. The comparison therefore straddles the α=0.05 threshold, and we report it as borderline — a numerical advantage that is not statistically significant at α=0.05 — rather than as an established one; a fortiori it is not significant after Bonferroni correction across the six ground-truth datasets (α≈0.008). At n=1000 indel the direction reverses, with FastTree2 significantly better (0.226 ± 0.015 vs 0.212 ± 0.014, p=1.5×10⁻⁴). 1. **Defective simulator.** Earlier versions reported Fusang 0.376 (clean) / 0.742 (indel) and Mash 0.394 / 0.762, with both methods degrading ~1.9×. **Metric and implementation stability.** Shuffling taxon order moves nRF by at most 0.015. DendroPy 5.0.8 (`treecompare.symmetric_difference` with `rooting='force-unrooted'`) agrees exactly with `nrf_standard.py` on 24 tree pairs (6 seeds × 2 conditions × 2 methods), and all 260 trees scored for this table were verified as fully resolved (197 = n−3 splits each).

Three findings emerge from the Mash benchmark:

1. **Mash is far less accurate than k-mer cosine on gene-length data, in both conditions.** On clean substitution-only sequences Mash gives 0.3486 ± 0.0351 against Fusang's 0.2364 ± 0.0336 — a gap of +0.1122 nRF (paired d=3.123, Fusang better in 30/30 seeds); on indel-rich data the gap widens to +0.2108 (0.4034 ± 0.0844 vs 0.1926 ± 0.0272, d=2.615, 30/30). The earlier claim that the two methods are roughly comparable was an artefact of the defective simulator, under which both were driven close to the random-tree ceiling and their differences compressed.

2. **The two methods respond to indels in opposite directions, and neither degrades severely.** Mash degrades significantly but modestly (0.3486 → 0.4034; per-seed ratio 1.164 ± 0.244; paired +0.0548, Wilcoxon p=3.0×10⁻³), while Fusang's spaced k-mer cosine significantly *improves* (0.2364 → 0.1926; ratio 0.827 ± 0.144; paired −0.0438, p=6.2×10⁻⁶) — consistent with the indel-rate scan in Table 3, where Fusang's advantage over FastTree2 grows with indel rate. The claim in earlier versions that both degrade ~1.9× is withdrawn. The mechanistic story changes accordingly: MinHash's deficit on gene-length sequences is a poor *baseline*, not exceptional indel sensitivity — at L=500 bp with k=21, nearly every 21-mer is unique, so Jaccard similarities saturate near zero and carry little signal whether or not indels are present. Indel robustness is a genuine advantage of k-mer cosine, but the dominant advantage here is absolute accuracy.

3. **MinHash Jaccard is not recommended for phylogenetic inference on gene-length sequences.** Mash was designed for whole-genome distance estimation, where genome-scale content masking makes MinHash effective. For gene-length sequences (L=500–1000 bp), the combination of short sequence length and a large k produces unreliable MinHash distances irrespective of indel content. K-mer cosine distances (Fusang) are the recommended alignment-free approach for phylogenetic inference at this scale.

### Indel robustness: Fusang advantage grows with indel rate

The accuracy ranking between Fusang and MSA-based methods changes under indels (Figure 1). On clean data, MSA methods hold a marginal advantage. As indel rate increases, MSA accuracy degrades systematically while Fusang's alignment-free distances remain more robust. The Fusang advantage increases with indel rate but crosses zero only between indel rates 0.01 and 0.02: Fusang is significantly *worse* than FastTree2 at 0.005 (−8.9%, p=1.75×10⁻¹¹) and at 0.01 (−5.1%, p=5.20×10⁻⁵), numerically better at 0.02 (+2.8%, p=0.0507 — borderline, not significant at α=0.05 under the zero-split convention used throughout), and clearly better at 0.05 (+10.9%, p=1.27×10⁻¹¹, Fusang better in 99/130 seeds) (Table 3). At the biologically realistic indel rate of 0.02, Fusang is numerically better than FastTree2 but not significantly so (TRUE-relative nRF: Fusang 0.199 ± 0.030 vs FastTree2 0.205 ± 0.030, 130 seeds, paired Wilcoxon p=0.0507, borderline).

### 130-seed benchmark validates indel robustness advantage

To rigorously assess the indel robustness, we conducted a **130-seed benchmark** (n=200, L=500 bp, indel rate=0.02, k=5,gap2) with paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test (Figure 2, Supplementary Figure S4, Supplementary Table S4). All 130 target seeds (100–229) produced valid tree files, and none exceeded the pre-specified nRF>0.3 outlier threshold, so the reported statistics use all 130 seeds without exclusion:

- **Overall (130 seeds, both methods evaluated against the simulated ground-truth tree)**: Fusang nRF=0.199 ± 0.030 vs FastTree2 nRF=0.205 ± 0.030; paired Cohen's d=−0.20 [95% bootstrap CI: −0.37, −0.03]; Fusang lower nRF in 71/130 seeds (54.6%), FastTree2 lower in 53, 6 ties

The 130-seed results show a directional advantage for Fusang that is weak and borderline (paired Wilcoxon p=0.0507 on integer RF counts with the zero-split convention; not significant at α=0.05). The bootstrap 95% confidence interval for Cohen's d excludes zero (95% CI for d = [−0.37, −0.03] in the Fusang − FastTree2 orientation), but |d|=0.20 is a small effect and the comparison does not survive Bonferroni correction across the six ground-truth datasets (α≈0.008); we therefore report it as borderline rather than decisive. This is substantially weaker than the p=1.9×10⁻⁷ with 88/130 wins reported in earlier versions of this manuscript, which were produced by a root-dependent nRF implementation (`calc_nrf_simple.py`) that systematically understated RF and biased comparisons in Fusang's favour and are withdrawn; under the root-independent definition now used throughout (see Methods), Fusang wins 71/130 seeds rather than 88/130. The direction of the conclusion is unchanged; its strength was previously overstated. (A separate earlier borderline result for this comparison — p=0.052 on 112 seeds — came from a legacy JC69 simulator with fixed branch lengths and is superseded by the present INDELible GTR+Γ birth–death protocol; see Supplementary Figure S6 for effect-size analyses across benchmarks.)

The per-seed nRF distributions reveal that Fusang's standard deviation (σ=0.030) is comparable to FastTree2's (σ=0.030), indicating stable performance across replicates. This contrasts with earlier DCM-based results that showed substantially larger Fusang variance due to EPA grafting instability. Per-seed values are provided in `indelible_n200_indel/bench_n200_indel_130seeds_nrfstandard.csv`.

### Multi-k distance ensemble matches the best single-k configuration and beats the default pipeline

Contiguous (non-spaced) k-mers are used for each individual k value to maximize information diversity; different k values capture signal at different spatial scales (shorter k: local conservation; longer k: extended sequence context). All configurations in Table 4 are evaluated on the same 130-seed INDELible benchmark (n=200, indel rate=0.02, seeds 100–229) and, except where noted, use neighbour-joining without NNI refinement so that distance-matrix effects are isolated from the tree-search refinement step.

### Multi-k NJ: comparison with MSA+ML

The datasets for this benchmark were regenerated with INDELible v1.03 under the same stated protocol, superseding data from an earlier hand-written coalescent simulator (see the provenance note to Table 5). The pipeline-level benchmark uses L=1000 bp rather than the L=500 bp of Tables 1 and 3: the coalescent protocol was fixed during multi-layer pipeline development — before the L1-vs-L3 comparison was designed — with L=1000 bp chosen so that the k=9 member of the multi-k set has non-sparse frequency vectors at n=200. In our harness the two are comparable in wall-clock time (median 44.5 s per seed for L1 versus 39.0 s for L3, both dominated by distance-matrix construction and alignment respectively rather than by tree search), so we do not claim a speed advantage for the ensemble at n=200; its advantages are the absence of an alignment step, the absence of manual k selection, and a cost profile that does not grow with alignment difficulty. The practical takeaway for exploratory analysis is a favourable speed–accuracy trade-off, not parity with MSA+ML.

### Boundary classifier accurately distinguishes dataset homogeneity

A critical component of Fusang's multi-layer pipeline is the Level 2 boundary classifier — a random forest (RF) model that determines whether a given node in the clustering hierarchy represents a homogeneous set of taxa (STOP) or requires further splitting (SPLIT). The classifier was trained on 8,116 labeled samples (6,176 SPLIT / 1,940 STOP; 585 simulated trees, zero generation failures) produced with the same INDELible v1.03 protocol as the accuracy benchmarks (birth–death tree prior λ=1.0/μ=0.5, GTR+Γ substitution, negative-binomial indels). We validated it on an independent test set of 150 scenarios spanning two fundamentally different tree types (Table 6; per-scenario results: Supplementary Table S15):

- **Coalescent trees** (75 scenarios): Single-population homogeneous simulations (n=50–500, 15 scenarios per scale) where all taxa evolve under the same model. These represent truly homogeneous datasets that should not be split.
- **Structured trees** (75 scenarios): Phylogenies with explicit structure, generated by concatenating 2–3 independently simulated clades whose substitution rates differ by a factor drawn from ×(0.5, 1.25, 2.0) (training data used a continuous ×U(0.4, 2.5) range), n=50–500. These represent datasets with genuine phylogenetic signal requiring SPLIT decisions.

**Classifier architecture.** The RF model uses a 50-dimensional feature vector derived from the k-mer distance matrix and tree topology, organized in seven groups: (A) cluster scale features (3: taxon count, sequence-length statistics); (B) pairwise k-mer distance distribution statistics (10: moments, quantiles, entropy); (C) multi-k distance agreement features (6: cross-k correlations and dispersions); (D) k-mer frequency spectrum features (8); (E) sequence-conservation features (4); (F) NJ tree topology features (6: branch-length moments, maximum branch length, cophenetic correlation); (G) global composition features (3), zero-padded to 50 dimensions. Samples comprise whole-tree feature vectors plus per-sub-cluster vectors extracted at internal clustering nodes (up to 15 sub-clusters per tree for n≤300; for computational feasibility at n=500 the cap was 5 — 585 trees and all whole-tree samples are complete regardless). Labels indicate whether the generating scenario was homogeneous (STOP) or structured (SPLIT). Five-fold cross-validation on the training set achieved ROC-AUC=0.831 ± 0.015 (held-out test: ROC-AUC=0.825, accuracy=0.801, F1=0.868). The lower CV AUC relative to an earlier JC69-era model (0.896) reflects that INDELible GTR+Γ data with variable-length unaligned sequences presents a harder decision boundary; the E2E results below show this does not compromise deployed accuracy. The model was implemented using scikit-learn (RandomForestClassifier, 200 estimators, max_depth=15, balanced class weights).

The predicted SPLIT probabilities are cleanly separated between the two classes (coalescent: p_split ∈ [0.005, 0.193], mean 0.077; structured: p_split ∈ [0.605, 1.000], mean 0.871), leaving a wide margin around the 0.5 decision threshold. The Wilson binomial confidence interval [0.975, 1.000] for the overall accuracy indicates that the true population accuracy is at least 97.5% with 95% confidence.

This validation addresses a critical concern in multi-layer phylogenetic pipelines: over-splitting of datasets that lack genuine phylogenetic structure. The random forest's ability to distinguish coalescent noise from structured signal — using features derived from k-mer distances, cluster size, and tree topology — prevents the pipeline from introducing spurious subdivisions that would degrade downstream accuracy. The perfect performance across 150 diverse simulated scenarios provides strong evidence that the boundary classifier generalizes beyond its training distribution. We note that test accuracy (100%) exceeds cross-validation performance on the training set (ROC-AUC=0.831); this gap reflects that the E2E test scenarios occupy well-separated regions of the parameter space (purely homogeneous vs clearly structured phylogenies), and performance on borderline scenarios near the STOP/SPLIT decision boundary may be lower. All test scenarios were generated using the same simulation engine (INDELible); validation on real biological datasets with known phylogenetic structure would further strengthen generalizability claims, though the diversity of simulation parameters (coalescent, structured, multiple substitution rates) provides meaningful coverage.

Three key findings emerge from this comparison:

1. **Co-phylog fails under indel-rich conditions.** Co-phylog's context-object approach (k=19) produces substantially worse trees (TRUE-relative mean nRF=0.8812 ± 0.0353, 4.4× worse than Fusang, 0/130 wins, p=4.35×10⁻²³, paired d=−15.40) on indel-rich data. The method relies on finding conserved 18-bp flanking contexts around individual positions; indels disrupt these contexts, causing significant loss of phylogenetic signal. This is a fundamental limitation of context-matching approaches when sequences contain insertions and deletions — precisely the conditions under which alignment-free methods are most needed. (Co-phylog results are from our Python reimplementation, Supplementary Note S4; on the fish benchmark — see Real genome benchmarks below — the reimplementation slightly exceeds the published configuration's accuracy, indicating the reimplementation is not disadvantaged relative to the original; the DNA conclusion here is therefore conservative in direction.)

2. **kmacs, the closest classical relative of our approach, is significantly less accurate under indels.** kmacs extends the average common substring (ACS) approach with k-mismatch matching, making it the most directly comparable classical method. We scanned k∈{3,5,10} and found accuracy degrades monotonically with increasing k (TRUE-relative: k=3 0.3656 ± 0.0389, k=5 0.4125 ± 0.0385, k=10 0.4517 ± 0.0399; all 0/130 wins vs Fusang); we report the best configuration (k=3). Even at its best, kmacs achieves nRF=0.3656 ± 0.0389, 1.8× worse than Fusang (paired Wilcoxon p=4.34×10⁻²³, paired d=−4.84) — though far more robust than Co-phylog (0.8812). Substring-matching distances are sensitive to indel-disrupted substring sharing even with k-mismatch tolerance, whereas k-mer frequency vectors degrade gracefully because indels perturb frequencies rather than destroy matches.

3. **Spaced k-mers keep — and strengthen — their advantage on indel-rich DNA, whereas NNI refinement adds no detectable gain, and two contiguous configurations beat the default pipeline at this scale.** Three separable results follow from Table 7.

   (i) *Spaced versus contiguous, like-for-like.* Comparing NJ trees with no NNI on either side, Fusang's spaced k=5,gap2 configuration (0.2003 ± 0.0296) significantly outperforms the contiguous k=5 cosine baseline (0.2078 ± 0.0314): spaced is more accurate on 75/130 seeds, contiguous on 46, with 9 ties, paired Wilcoxon p=5.54×10⁻⁴, paired d=−0.337. Under the superseded nRF implementation we reported 68/130 and p=7.97×10⁻³, so the advantage survives the switch to the standard root-independent RF and is if anything slightly stronger. It remains frame-specific: on the same 130 seeds the FT2-relative version of the identical contrast is not significant (spaced better in 60, contiguous in 63, 7 ties, p=0.42), continuing the pattern — already seen in our earlier underpowered 27-seed FT2-relative test (p=0.26) — that the FT2 reference frame masks real differences. On the protein benchmark the spaced advantage we previously reported (p=0.0078) does not survive the metric change (Table 10; p=0.64 and p=0.094), so the evidence for spaced patterns in this work rests on indel-rich DNA alone and we do not claim that gap content alone predicts a spaced-pattern gain. The DNA result is consistent with the proposed mechanism — spaced patterns recover k-mers that would otherwise be destroyed by an insertion or deletion falling inside their span, so their benefit should grow with indel content.

   (ii) *NNI refinement provides no significant gain.* The full pipeline (0.1990 ± 0.0296) is not significantly better than the pure-NJ spaced tree (0.2003 ± 0.0296): full pipeline better in 54 seeds, NJ-only better in 50, 26 ties, paired Wilcoxon p=0.34, paired d=−0.092. The claim in earlier versions that BME-scored NNI refinement contributes a further significant gain (better on 100/130 seeds, p=7.1×10⁻¹³, d=0.76) is **withdrawn**: it was produced by the superseded nRF implementation. NNI refinement remains in the default pipeline as a low-cost, never-harmful-on-average step, but at L=500 bp we can no longer claim it improves accuracy.

   (iii) *An honest negative for the default configuration.* At gene length (L=500 bp) two configurations are significantly more accurate than the default Fusang pipeline: contiguous k=7 (0.1938 ± 0.0295; k=7 better in 76/130 seeds, Fusang better in 42, 12 ties, p=3.9×10⁻³, d=+0.252) and the multi-k ensemble (0.1940 ± 0.0296; ensemble better in 72/130, Fusang better in 46, 12 ties, p=7.7×10⁻³, d=+0.246), while contiguous k=9 (0.2005) is statistically indistinguishable from it (p=0.377). We therefore no longer describe the default spaced k=5 + BME-NNI configuration as the most accurate Fusang setting at this scale: it is a robust default, but users seeking maximum accuracy on gene-length data of this divergence should prefer contiguous k=7 or the multi-k ensemble (Table 4; see also Practical recommendations).

**Multi-k ensemble.** On this 130-seed INDELible benchmark the multi-k ensemble (contiguous k=5,7,9 average, NJ) achieves TRUE-relative nRF=0.1940 ± 0.0296 — significantly better than the contiguous k=5 baseline (0.2078; ensemble better in 93/130, p=2.68×10⁻¹², d=0.755), than contiguous k=9 (0.2005; 81/130, p=9.04×10⁻⁸, d=0.517), than the NJ-only spaced configuration (0.2003; 74/130, p=1.16×10⁻³, d=0.317) and than the full Fusang pipeline (0.1990; 72/130, p=7.7×10⁻³, d=0.246), while remaining statistically indistinguishable from contiguous k=7 (0.1938; 48/130, p=0.669). In contrast to earlier versions of this manuscript, we therefore claim an accuracy advantage of the multi-k ensemble over the default spaced configuration at L=500 bp — a small one (0.0050 nRF, d=0.246), but significant and reproducible across 130 seeds. Table 4 examines the ensemble against its single-k components in detail. The ensemble's other established benefits are (i) the single-k→multi-k gain under the L=1000 bp protocol (Table 5, d=0.67, p=0.0018; an 8.7% relative reduction in nRF, 0.150→0.137) and (ii) the automatic rescue of the saturating gene-scale default at genome scale (see Real genome benchmarks below).

**Regarding andi** [25]: andi uses suffix-array-based anchor distances designed for genome-scale sequences (>10kb). On gene-length sequences (~500 bp), andi's anchor-finding mechanism has insufficient maximal unique matches for reliable distance estimation. In our harness andi returned no usable tree on the gene-length families we attempted, so we report **no accuracy figure** for it — the run record (`benchmark_andi_results.json`) captures timings only, with null tree fields for every replicate. We therefore characterise this as a design-domain mismatch rather than a measured accuracy result. This is consistent with andi's intended application to bacterial genome phylogenomics and does not reflect a methodological weakness. We note that andi and Co-phylog represent fundamentally different alignment-free paradigms — suffix-array anchors and context-object matching, respectively — and neither is directly comparable to Fusang's k-mer frequency vector approach in both methodology and intended scale.

**Regarding Skmer** [29]: Skmer estimates distances from genome skims through a k-mer depth-spectrum coverage and error-correction model. On our 500-bp single-gene benchmark data (INDELible n=200), Skmer *completes* without error at both the default k=31 and at k=21, but its coverage model is inoperative: every sequence reports "coverage too low, not able to estimate it; no correction applied", because at gene length each k-mer occurs approximately once (0/200 sequences contain any repeated 21-mer or 31-mer), producing degenerate depth-1 histograms on which the coverage model cannot operate. The resulting uncorrected distance matrices are dominated by capped sentinel values (45.1% of off-diagonal pairs at k=31; 5.6% at k=21), and NJ trees built from them score nRF=0.782 (k=31) / 0.614 (k=21) against the seed-100 true tree under the standard root-independent unrooted RF (rescoring: `repro_ablations/skmer_probe_nrfstandard.py`; the earlier values 0.320/0.315, produced by the superseded root-dependent nRF implementation, are withdrawn) — 2.2–2.9× Fusang's error on the same seed (0.274) and worse than every tested competitor except Co-phylog. Skmer is therefore functionally inapplicable to gene-length phylogenetic inference — an outcome parallel to andi, whose suffix-array anchors likewise require genome-scale input. Both cases reflect design-domain mismatches rather than methodological weaknesses. (Versioning note: the bioconda package labelled skmer 3.3.0 ships skmer 3.1.0 code; we verified both environments install byte-identical `__main__.py`. An earlier record of a division-by-zero failure traced to a now-superseded dataset whose depth histograms contained interior zero counts; on the current benchmark data the estimator's graceful "coverage too low" branch is taken instead. ### Accuracy at scale: n=1000 indel performance

On indel-rich data at n=1000 (30 seeds, sub=0.05, indel=0.02), Fusang's simplified pipeline (k-mer→cosine→NJ plus BME-scored NNI refinement for n≥100) achieves TRUE-relative nRF=0.226 ± 0.015 vs FastTree2 0.212 ± 0.014 (paired Wilcoxon p=1.5×10⁻⁴; FastTree2 is significantly better, and the earlier claim of statistical indistinguishability — 0.133 ± 0.010 vs 0.131 ± 0.009, p=0.38 — is withdrawn as a product of the superseded nRF implementation; Table 1). The marginal, non-significant Fusang advantage observed at n=200 (p=0.0507) therefore reverses with increasing taxon count rather than extrapolating to n=1000: the indel-robustness benefit is confined to gene-family scale (n=200), while the FastTree2 advantage on clean data is significant at every scale tested (n=500: p=1.7×10⁻⁶; n=1000: p=1.7×10⁻⁶). This scale-dependence is itself practically informative: alignment-free inference is most competitive exactly in the regime where indels are frequent and taxon counts are moderate.

### Validation on real 16S rRNA sequences

Fusang processed the 73 analysed 16S rRNA sequences without alignment (simplified pipeline, k=5,gap2) in 0.108 s for the k-mer counting plus NJ stage. Tree-based pairwise distances were compared against NCBI taxonomic classifications (Table 9). For reference-tree comparisons we rebuilt the alignment-based comparator end-to-end: the archived alignment file was found to be not genuinely aligned (pairwise identity between the two *Escherichia*/*Bacillus* sequences fell below the 0.25 random baseline), so all sequences were realigned with MAFFT 7.526 (--localpair) and the FastTree2 tree rebuilt from it. Fusang vs this rebuilt FastTree2 tree gives nRF=0.371 — substantial but no longer near-total topological disagreement.

We evaluated both trees against the NCBI taxonomy as an external reference. A taxonomic group is scored as recovered when the MRCA of all tips carrying the label has exactly that tip set. Against the true taxonomy (13 phyla, 7 with ≥2 taxa; 40 orders, 14 with ≥2), the Fusang tree recovers 3/7 assessable phyla and 10/14 assessable orders as monophyletic, while the rebuilt FastTree2 tree recovers 6/7 phyla and 11/14 orders. The RF distance against a taxonomy-derived reference tree (phylum→order→taxon containment hierarchy, polytomous where unresolved) is nRF=0.414 for Fusang vs 0.343 for FastTree2 under the conventional RF/(2n−6) normalisation (0.547 vs 0.453 under the resolution-aware RF/(s₁+s₂) normalisation; both normalisations rank the two trees identically). While Fusang detects substantial genuine signal (Table 9), it falls short of MSA-based accuracy on this dataset (Figure 6, Supplementary Figure S5). This result delineates an applicability boundary of the method rather than contradicting the indel-robustness findings on simulated data: 16S rRNA genes contain highly conserved regions where positional homology from alignment provides strong phylogenetic signal that k-mer frequency vectors, which discard positional information, cannot fully capture.

1. **K-mer frequency methods outperform context-matching on protein sequences.** The best k-mer configuration achieves mean nRF=0.329, while Co-phylog's best configuration (halfctx=11) achieves nRF=0.727 — a 2.2× accuracy advantage (paired Wilcoxon p=0.0010, Cohen's d=−3.47) — and its default (halfctx=5) achieves nRF=0.786, a 2.4× gap (p=0.0020, d=−2.28). This extends our observation from DNA data (Table 7) to protein sequences, confirming that k-mer frequency vectors capture phylogenetic signal more robustly than Co-phylog's context-object matching across both sequence alphabets. Both gaps are markedly wider than the 1.5–1.8× we previously reported from a root-dependent nRF implementation (see Materials and Methods); the direction of the conclusion is unchanged, its magnitude was previously understated.

2. **No spaced-pattern advantage is detectable on protein data.** Under the root-dependent nRF implementation used previously we reported a significant spaced-pattern gain on this benchmark (spaced k=4,gap1 vs contiguous k=4: p=0.0078, better in 8/11 families). Recomputed with the standard root-independent unrooted RF, that gain disappears: pattern `1011` (0.354 ± 0.158) is marginally *worse* than contiguous k=4 (0.346 ± 0.169; paired Wilcoxon p=0.64), and pattern `11011` (0.329 ± 0.148) is not significantly better than contiguous k=5 (0.343 ± 0.156; p=0.094). We therefore **withdraw** the protein spaced-pattern claim: it was an artefact of the biased metric. On indel-rich DNA the spaced advantage survives the same metric change and is if anything stronger (Table 7, Finding 3: 75/130 seeds, p=5.54×10⁻⁴, paired d=0.337), so the evidence for spaced patterns in this work rests on the DNA benchmark alone, and we no longer claim that spaced sampling helps wherever gap content is high — the SwissTree alignments are strongly gap-rich (median gap fraction 50%, range 11–81%) yet show no such benefit.

3. **The choice of representation and distance metric dominates; pattern choice is secondary.** Across both DNA (Table 7) and protein (Table 10) benchmarks, the choice of representation and distance metric (k-mer frequency cosine vs context-object matching vs MinHash Jaccard) has a far larger effect than the choice between spaced and contiguous patterns: on protein data the k-mer-cosine vs Co-phylog contrast spans |d|≈2.2–3.5, whereas spaced vs contiguous is non-significant (finding 2). The systematic evaluation of k-mer frequency vector cosine distances — across spaced and contiguous patterns and across sequence alphabets — is the core contribution of this work.

2. **The k parameter must scale with sequence length, and the multi-k ensemble provides this automatically.** The gene-scale default (k=5) saturates at genome scale (only 1,024 possible 5-mers over 17–4,600 kb of sequence), producing near-random trees (fish nRF=0.545; E. coli nRF=0.875). Single larger k values recover signal on fish (k=9: 0.091; k=11: 0.091), yet the multi-k average — which includes the saturated k=5 and k=7 matrices — still achieves the best result (0.045). A plausible but unverified mechanism (this is a single dataset, and the explanation is post hoc) is that the informative scale-specific variation in the k=7–11 matrices dominates the topology while the ensemble averages out scale-specific noise. The multi-k ensemble thereby removes the need for length-aware k selection across a >2,000-fold range of sequence lengths (500 bp genes to 17 kb mitogenomes).

3. **E. coli/Shigella delineates an applicability boundary: shallow divergence dominated by recombination and gene flux.** On the 27-genome dataset (within-species divergence, recombination and gene gain/loss dominate over substitution), k-mer cosine plateaus at nRF=0.50 for all k≥9 and all multi-k variants, and kmacs (k=3; run left overnight, not instrumented for timing) performs no better (nRF=0.542), whereas Mash reaches 0.208 (our run) / 0.12 (published), and anchor-based methods reach 0.08 (published andi, Co-phylog, phylonium). At within-species identity, the frequency-vector difference between genomes is dominated by the conserved background, while anchor- and sketch-based distances (shared k-mer fractions, average common substrings) remain sensitive to the small divergent fraction — note that kmacs, despite being substring-based, also fails to exploit this fraction at its gene-scale k=3 configuration. We conclude that k-mer cosine distances are the method of choice for moderate-to-deep divergence (gene families across genera to phyla, organelle genomes, the simulated 5%-substitution regime), and recommend anchor-based or MinHash methods for strain-level bacterial collections — a boundary that complements the 16S structured-gene boundary reported above.

### Gene-length validation on real DNA: 13 mitochondrial loci

The whole-genome benchmarks above leave the simulated gene-length regime (L=500 bp) untested on real DNA. We therefore extracted the 13 mitochondrial protein-coding genes (ND1–6, ND4L, COX1–3, ATP6/8, CYTB; 168–1,866 bp, 5 of 13 loci within the 500–1,000 bp band of the simulation benchmarks) from the same 25 fish genomes and ran per-gene benchmarks against a MAFFT+FastTree2 anchor, scoring ETE3 nRF both against the Fischer et al. whole-mitogenome reference tree and — more informatively — as direct Fusang-vs-FastTree2 topological agreement (Supplementary Table S17).

Per-gene gene-tree discordance dominates reference-relative scores: even the MAFFT+FastTree2 anchor differs from the whole-mitogenome reference by nRF=0.332±0.175, so no per-gene tree can be expected to match the full-genome phylogeny. Against this background, the multi-k ensemble (mean of k=5,7,9 cosine distances) agrees with the MSA+ML anchor at nRF=0.451±0.127 across the 13 loci. Reference-relative scores show the same k-dependence as in simulation: the multi-k ensemble (0.535±0.113) and single k=7 (0.532±0.136) perform comparably, and both outperform the gene-scale default k=5 (0.601±0.107). Agreement is length-dependent: loci ≥1 kb show the closest Fusang–FastTree2 agreement (mean nRF=0.37, n=5), while the three shortest loci (ATP8 168 bp, ND4L 297 bp, ND3 350 bp; mean nRF=0.59) are poorly resolved by both method families. Because mitochondrial genes evolve under a low-indel regime (per-locus alignment gap fractions 0–1.7%), this benchmark complements the indel-rich whole-genome test of Table 12: it probes the low-indel gene-length regime on real DNA, and its interpretation is agreement with the MSA+ML reference pipeline rather than absolute accuracy. We note that this is a single locus set from one mitogenome clade; broader gene-family validation remains future work.

## DISCUSSION

### K-mer frequency vectors for indel-rich phylogenetics

The central contribution of this work is the systematic evaluation of k-mer frequency vector cosine distances for phylogenetic inference under indel-rich conditions, delivered as an open-source tool (Fusang: Tardigrade Edition) with automatic parameter selection, a multi-k ensemble requiring no manual k choice, and an empirically delimited domain of applicability. Spaced k-mers were introduced by PatternHunter in 2002 [30] for sequence alignment and have been validated in protein classification, metagenomics, and genome assembly — yet their application to phylogenetic inference remained almost entirely unexplored. Fusang: Tardigrade Edition investigates both spaced and contiguous k-mer patterns within the cosine distance framework, finding that:

1. **Cosine distance is the primary accuracy driver; the spaced pattern provides a significant but modest secondary gain, whereas NNI refinement does not.** On the 130-seed INDELible benchmark, the like-for-like NJ comparison shows spaced k=5,gap2 significantly outperforming contiguous k=5 cosine (TRUE-relative 0.2003 vs 0.2078, spaced better on 75/130 seeds, paired Wilcoxon p=5.54×10⁻⁴, paired d=0.337) — our earlier 27-seed FT2-relative test was underpowered (p=0.26), and even on all 130 seeds the FT2-relative version of the identical contrast is not significant (p=0.42), illustrating how that reference frame masks real differences. BME-scored NNI refinement, by contrast, adds no detectable gain over the pure-NJ spaced tree (0.1990 vs 0.2003, p=0.34); the significant gain reported in earlier versions (p=7.1×10⁻¹³) is withdrawn. The spaced-pattern benefit is confined to indel-rich DNA: on the gap-rich SwissTree protein benchmark the advantage we previously reported (p=0.0078) was an artefact of a root-dependent nRF implementation and disappears under the standard metric (Table 10; spaced vs contiguous p=0.64 and p=0.094 for the two patterns tested), so gap content alone does not predict a spaced-pattern gain. On indel-rich DNA a wider gap (gap3) improves over gap2 by 10.5% (Supplementary Table S2). Finally, at this scale the choice of k matters more than either of these refinements: contiguous k=7 (0.1938) and the multi-k ensemble (0.1940) are both significantly more accurate than the default spaced+NNI pipeline (0.1990; p=3.9×10⁻³ and p=7.7×10⁻³), so we present the default configuration as robust rather than optimal. The systematic evaluation of k-mer frequency vector cosine distances across patterns and sequence types is the core methodological contribution.

2. **Indel robustness and absolute accuracy are separate properties, and MinHash's deficit at gene length is the latter.** On indel-rich data Mash (MinHash Jaccard, 30 seeds) produces trees at nRF=0.4034 ± 0.0844 versus 0.1926 ± 0.0272 for k-mer cosine (both vs the true tree). Decomposing this: under indels Mash degrades significantly but modestly (0.3486 → 0.4034, per-seed ratio 1.164 ± 0.244, p=3.0×10⁻³), whereas k-mer cosine *improves* (0.2364 → 0.1926, ratio 0.827 ± 0.144, p=6.2×10⁻⁶) — so the two are not "both degrading by the same factor, with cosine marginally ahead", as an earlier version of this manuscript claimed on the basis of defective simulator data (1.93× vs 1.97×, absolute values 0.762 vs 0.742); that claim is withdrawn together with the underlying data. Mash's problem on 500-bp sequences is a poor baseline, not exceptional indel sensitivity: with k=21 nearly every 21-mer in a 500-bp sequence is unique, so Jaccard similarities saturate near zero and the sketch carries little signal whether or not indels are present. Positional information is indeed discarded by MinHash, but at this sequence length the dominant term is sketch sparsity rather than indel-induced disruption.

3. **The distance metric, the k-mer representation and the value of k must all fit the data.** On clean gene-length data, MinHash Jaccard (nRF=0.3486 ± 0.0351, 30 seeds) is 1.5× worse than k-mer cosine (0.2364 ± 0.0336) — a large, not marginal, gap (paired d=3.12, Fusang better in 30/30 seeds). On indel data the gap widens further (0.4034 vs 0.1926, d=2.61). Within the k-mer family itself the choice of k is not neutral: at L=500 bp contiguous k=7 (0.1938) and the multi-k ensemble (0.1940) both beat the default spaced k=5 configuration (0.1990) significantly, while k=5 (0.2078) is clearly worst. This underscores that "alignment-free" is not a monolithic category: method and parameter selection should be guided by the expected evolutionary process and data characteristics, sketch-based distances tuned for whole genomes should not be assumed transferable to gene-length input, and a fixed default k should not be assumed optimal across sequence lengths and divergence levels.

### From Fusang v1 to Tardigrade Edition: an architectural evolution

The Fusang lineage illustrates a methodological evolution from specialized to general-purpose phylogenetic inference. Our v1 [6] established proof-of-concept: alignment-free phylogenetics can work, but was limited to 4–40 taxa and required pre-trained deep learning models. The Tardigrade Edition represents a complete re-architecture:
- **Representation**: neural network features → spaced k-mer frequency vectors
- **Scalability**: 40 taxa maximum → 10,000+ taxa
- **Speed**: GPU-dependent inference → CPU-only, minutes for thousands of taxa
- **Deployment**: Docker/cloud requirement → single binary + Python script
- **Indel handling**: no explicit indel modeling → hypothesized robustness from spaced sampling (empirically modest at indel rate 0.02; see Results)

This evolution demonstrates that feature engineering — specifically, **k-mer frequency vector cosine distances** — can match or exceed learned representations for phylogenetic inference while providing order-of-magnitude improvements in speed, scalability, and accessibility. The key insight is that k-mer frequency vectors with cosine distance preserve sufficient phylogenetic signal for accurate distance-based tree inference, while spaced sampling is hypothesized to provide additional robustness against indel-induced length variation (an advantage that is empirically modest at the tested indel rate).

### The role of pipeline simplicity in phylogenetic accuracy

The discovery that the simplified pipeline (nRF=0.081, illustrative single seed: n=200, indel rate=0.02, seed 42, standard root-independent unrooted RF) dramatically outperforms the full DCM pipeline (nRF=0.096–0.523 across eleven max_group_size settings on the same seed) at n≤200 has important implications. It challenges the assumption that methodological complexity — more sophisticated clustering, evolutionary placement, post-processing refinement — necessarily improves accuracy. In Fusang's case, the EPA grafting step introduces topological errors that dominate the signal, particularly when grafting subtrees with internal structure onto a fixed backbone.

This finding is consistent with a broader pattern in computational biology: simpler models often outperform complex ones when the underlying signal is weak or noisy. The k-mer frequency vectors from n=200 taxa contain sufficient phylogenetic signal for direct distance-based tree building; adding intermediate transformations only amplifies noise.

We therefore recommend the simplified pipeline as the default for n≤500 and DCM for larger datasets, where it provides a modest accuracy advantage (n=1000: DCM nRF=0.083 ± 0.005, 9 seeds 401–409, vs simplified nRF=0.102 on seed 401 — obtained under the superseded nRF implementation; the seed 401–409 true trees are no longer in the working tree, so this comparison could not be recomputed with the standard unrooted RF, and it is used here only to document scalability, not as an accuracy result) in addition to the scalability needed when pairwise distance computation (scaling as O(n²)) becomes the computational bottleneck.

### Limitations and future work

Several limitations of the current study warrant discussion.

**Scale-dependent accuracy**: On clean (no-indel) data, MSA-based methods maintain a clear advantage at every scale tested. A 30-seed benchmark at n=500 shows Fusang nRF=0.244 ± 0.019 vs FastTree2 nRF=0.213 ± 0.017 (paired Cohen's d=2.17, Wilcoxon p=1.7×10⁻⁶); at n=1000 the gap widens to 0.257 ± 0.019 vs 0.212 ± 0.016 (d=3.50, p=1.7×10⁻⁶); and at n=200 FastTree2 is also significantly better (0.234 ± 0.035 vs 0.210 ± 0.034, p=2.07×10⁻¹⁴). After Bonferroni correction across the six ground-truth datasets (α≈0.008), every clean-data comparison remains significant in favour of FastTree2. This is expected: on clean data without indels, alignment-based ML methods benefit from full positional information. **Pipeline caveat on cross-scale comparisons.** Fusang switches automatically to the DCM+EPA pipeline for n > 500 (`SIMPLE_THRESHOLD = 500`), so the n=1000 values in Table 1 were produced by DCM+EPA whereas the n=200 and n=500 values were produced by the simplified pipeline; the cross-scale trend therefore conflates the effect of this pipeline switch with any effect of taxon count, and should not be interpreted as the pure scaling behaviour of a single pipeline. Fusang's only statistically significant advantages in this study are on indel-rich data at indel rate 0.05 (n=200), and even there the effect is small; at the biologically realistic rate of 0.02 the n=200 difference is numerical only and not significant at α=0.05 (p=0.0507).

On indel-rich data the advantage does not scale up: at n=500 indel the two methods are statistically indistinguishable (Fusang 0.208 ± 0.019 vs FastTree2 0.209 ± 0.017, paired Wilcoxon p=0.29, d=−0.07), and at n=1000 indel FastTree2 is significantly better (Fusang 0.226 ± 0.015 vs 0.212 ± 0.014, p=1.5×10⁻⁴, d=0.98). The marginal n=200 indel advantage (p=0.0507, borderline at α=0.05) therefore reverses with scale rather than growing. The simplified pipeline is the default for n≤500; for n>500, DCM+EPA provides essential scalability and a small accuracy advantage (direct comparison at n=1000: DCM nRF=0.083 ± 0.005, 9 seeds 401–409, vs simplified nRF=0.102 on seed 401 — obtained under the superseded nRF implementation; the seed 401–409 true trees are no longer in the working tree, so this comparison could not be recomputed with the standard unrooted RF, and it is used here only to document scalability, not as an accuracy result; an earlier threshold of 2,000 forced the simplified pipeline at that scale, degraded accuracy, and was reverted). A systematic multi-seed accuracy comparison across the n=500–2,000 transition zone remains future work.

**Simulated-to-real transfer**: While 16S rRNA validation (73 analysed taxa, 13 phyla under corrected NCBI Taxonomy annotation; Supplementary Note S17) confirms that Fusang detects biological phylogenetic signal — same-rank tree distances are significantly reduced at every level (phylum 31.4% to genus 78.6%, all p<1×10⁻⁴) and 3/7 assessable phyla plus 10/14 assessable orders are recovered as monophyletic — accuracy on this structured RNA gene is lower than MSA-based methods (Fusang vs taxonomy nRF=0.414 vs FastTree2 vs taxonomy nRF=0.343, RF/(2n−6) normalisation). The nRF=0.371 between Fusang and the rebuilt FastTree2 tree reflects the substantial divergence between k-mer frequency and alignment-based phylogenetic inference on this sequence type. 16S rRNA genes, with their mixture of highly conserved stems and variable loops, favor methods that exploit positional homology; k-mer frequency vectors, which discard positional information, are inherently disadvantaged on such structured sequences. The AFproject SwissTree protein benchmark (Table 10) provides additional cross-domain validation: k-mer frequency methods achieve mean nRF=0.329 on real protein gene trees (11 families, 21–159 taxa; median alignment gap content 50%, range 11–81%), while Co-phylog's best configuration (halfctx=11) achieves nRF=0.727 (paired Wilcoxon p=0.0010, Cohen's d=−3.47 vs Fusang pattern `11011`) and its default (halfctx=5) achieves nRF=0.786 (p=0.0020, d=−2.28) — confirming that k-mer approaches transfer robustly from simulated DNA to real, strongly indel-rich protein data, while also making clear that the absolute error of every method on this benchmark is high (nRF≈0.33 for the best configuration). On BAliBASE v3.0 protein alignments (20 families) the picture is markedly less favourable, and it reverses a claim made in earlier versions of this manuscript: Fusang and FastTree2 trees share almost no topology (median nRF=0.982, mean 0.826; only 3/20 families below 0.5, of which two are exact matches — Supplementary Table S7). We previously reported "competitive performance with 65% of families below nRF 0.5 (median 0.45)"; that statement was produced by the root-dependent nRF implementation described in Materials and Methods and is withdrawn. Two caveats bound the interpretation: this is a Fusang-vs-FastTree2 *agreement* statistic, not an accuracy measurement against a trusted reference (BAliBASE provides alignments, not reference trees), so it cannot establish which method is wrong; and the mechanism is plausible — short protein sequences (111–576 aa) over a 20-letter alphabet give sparse k-mer frequency vectors very little signal, consistent with the length-dependence we observe on the mitochondrial loci. We therefore report BAliBASE as an applicability boundary rather than as evidence of competitive accuracy. Real genome-scale validation on AFproject community datasets (Table 12) shows the multi-k ensemble reaching the community-benchmark ceiling on deep-divergence indel-rich mitochondrial genomes (nRF=0.045, matched by all top methods), but also delineates a boundary: on shallow-divergence E. coli/Shigella genomes where recombination and gene flux dominate, k-mer cosine (nRF=0.50) is outperformed by Mash (0.12–0.21) and anchor-based methods (published 0.08). The gene-length DNA regime of the simulation benchmarks is now covered by the 13-locus mitochondrial benchmark above (168–1,866 bp), which shows moderate Fusang–FastTree2 agreement (multi-k nRF=0.451±0.127) under a low-indel regime with gene-tree discordance as the dominant error source; real gene-length DNA with documented indel-rich evolution and trusted trees remains an open validation target, with candidate data types including viral quasispecies collections and rapidly evolving gene families with curated reference trees.

**Fixed k and gap**: The current implementation uses a static k and gap for the entire dataset. On the 130-seed INDELible benchmark (Table 4) the multi-k ensemble (nRF=0.1940) is statistically indistinguishable from the best single contiguous configuration (k=7, 0.1938; p=0.669) and significantly better than the default spaced+NNI pipeline (0.1990; p=0.0077, d=0.246) and than k=5 (0.2078; p=2.68×10⁻¹²) and k=9 (0.2005; p=9.04×10⁻⁸), so at L=500 bp it is the most accurate configuration we tested while requiring no manual k selection. That the ensemble never beats the best single k by a significant margin indicates limited complementary signal beyond what a well-chosen single k captures at this sequence length — the gain over the default is real but small (0.0050 nRF). Because the ranking of k values is likely to shift with sequence length, divergence and taxon count, we do not treat k=7 as a universal optimum. Further optimization, such as weighted fusion, inclusion of spaced k-mers in the ensemble, or per-cluster parameter selection, may yield additional gains. The optimal set of k values and fusion weights has not been systematically explored.

**Distance metric exploration**: Cosine distance and Jensen-Shannon divergence represent points in a larger space of possible metrics on k-mer frequency vectors. Earth mover's distance, learned embeddings, or information-theoretic metrics may capture additional phylogenetic signal. Our own cosine-vs-JSD comparison was inconclusive (Methods), and on the 130-seed benchmark the choice of k (k=7 0.1938 vs k=5 0.2078) moved accuracy more than any metric or refinement choice we tested, so we regard the exploration of k and fusion strategies as at least as promising as the exploration of metrics.

**L3 protocol and portability**: The definitive L3 (MAFFT+FastTree2) comparison is now based on a complete 30-seed run executed on Linux (WSL2 Ubuntu-24.04) with an explicitly nucleotide FastTree2 protocol (`-nt -gtr -nosupport`); an earlier 5-seed Windows run was affected both by MAFFT instability (empty alignments on 25/30 seeds) and by an inadvertently omitted `-nt` flag (protein-mode distance settings on DNA input), and is superseded (Supplementary Note S8). Both L1 (nRF=0.137) and L3 (nRF=0.124) remain distant from the true tree (~12–15% bipartition error), with L3 significantly better; the protocol is less severe than the one underlying the superseded figures (~58–60%), which we withdraw as defective (see Table 5). The Linux requirement itself is a practical portability constraint: native-Windows MAFFT proved unreliable at this scale.

**Mash multi-seed benchmark**: The Mash benchmark (Table 2) is based on 30 seeds (seeds 100–129, this work, WSL2 Ubuntu 24.04, Mash v2.3) at a single indel rate (0.02) and a single sequence length (L=500 bp); a 130-seed sensitivity run over the full seed range gives the same picture. On the replacement INDELible data Mash degrades modestly but significantly under indels (0.3486 → 0.4034, per-seed ratio 1.164 ± 0.244, p=3.0×10⁻³) while Fusang's k-mer cosine improves (0.2364 → 0.1926, ratio 0.827 ± 0.144, p=6.2×10⁻⁶). The earlier figures (Mash 0.762 ± 0.036, 1.93× degradation; k-mer cosine degrading comparably at 1.97×) came from the defective hand-written coalescent simulator and from Mash runs that omitted the required `-i` flag, and are withdrawn (see the Table 2 provenance note). Because only one indel rate and one sequence length were tested, we cannot yet locate the sequence length or indel density at which MinHash begins to collapse; a scan across indel rates 0.005–0.05 remains future work.

**Sequence length**: All simulated benchmarks used a fixed sequence length of L=500 bp, representative of typical gene-length sequences. K-mer frequency estimation accuracy depends on sequence length — longer sequences provide more robust frequency estimates, while very short sequences (e.g., L<200 bp, typical of amplicon data) may yield noisier k-mer profiles. Cross-validation on the AFproject SwissTree dataset (protein sequences, 111–576 amino acids; Table 10) confirms that Fusang's performance transfers across a range of sequence lengths, but systematic evaluation at extreme lengths (L=100–200 bp and L>2000 bp) remains future work.

**Boundary classifier data independence**: The 150 E2E test scenarios were generated using simulation parameters distinct from the training data, but all scenarios used the same simulation engine (INDELible). The 100% accuracy, while statistically supported (Wilson CI [0.975, 1.0] for n=150), may overestimate real-world performance where data distributions differ from simulation. Internal cross-validation on the training set (n=8,116, 5-fold CV, ROC-AUC=0.831 ± 0.015) confirms the classifier generalizes within its simulation domain. Validation on real biological datasets with known phylogenetic structure would strengthen generalizability claims. The classifier's feature importance ranking (Supplementary Note S9) shows that k-mer distance entropy and cluster size are the most discriminative features, with tree topology metrics providing secondary signal. A systematic feature ablation study quantifying each feature group's causal contribution to classification accuracy remains future work.

**Current status of n=500/n=1000 benchmarks**: Multi-seed benchmarking at n=500 and n=1000 (30 seeds each) has been completed for both clean and indel-rich data, all TRUE-relative. At n=1000 indel, FastTree2 is significantly better (Fusang 0.226 ± 0.015 vs 0.212 ± 0.014, p=1.5×10⁻⁴, paired d=0.98), reversing the marginal n=200 indel advantage; at n=500 indel the two are indistinguishable (0.208 ± 0.019 vs 0.209 ± 0.017, p=0.29). A multiple-comparison correction across the six ground-truth datasets confirms that on clean data FastTree2 significantly outperforms Fusang at every scale (n=200: p=2.07×10⁻¹⁴; n=500: p=1.7×10⁻⁶; n=1000: p=1.7×10⁻⁶; all survive Bonferroni α≈0.008), while on indel-rich data FastTree2 is significantly better at n=1000 (p=1.5×10⁻⁴), the two are tied at n=500 and n=500-indel, and Fusang is numerically better only at n=200 (p=0.0507, which is not significant at α=0.05 and does not survive correction) (Supplementary Table S8).

**Comparison with classical alignment-free methods**: We have completed systematic head-to-head comparison with four classical alignment-free methods — andi [25], Co-phylog [26], kmacs [12], and Skmer [29] — across simulated DNA (Table 7, 130 INDELible seeds, TRUE-relative) and real protein data (Table 10, AFproject SwissTree, 11 gene families). K-mer frequency methods consistently outperform the alternatives: Co-phylog's context-matching approach fails under indels (DNA TRUE-relative nRF=0.8812 ± 0.0353, 4.4× worse than Fusang's 0.1990, 0/130 wins, paired d=−15.40) and on protein data (best config halfctx=11 nRF=0.727 vs Fusang nRF=0.329, d=−3.47, p=0.0010; its default halfctx=5 nRF=0.786, d=−2.28, p=0.0020); kmacs (best of k∈{3,5,10}) is significantly less accurate under indels (TRUE-relative nRF=0.3656 ± 0.0389 vs Fusang 0.1990, 0/130 wins, p=4.34×10⁻²³, paired d=−4.84; k=5 0.4125 and k=10 0.4517 are worse still); while andi (suffix-array anchors) and Skmer (genome-skim coverage model; completes on 500-bp inputs but the coverage correction is inoperative — degenerate depth-1 histograms, 45% of distances capped at the sentinel value, nRF≈0.32 on the probe seed) are structurally inapplicable to gene-length sequences by design. This comparison also produced a negative result for our own default: on the same 130 seeds contiguous k=7 (0.1938) and the multi-k ensemble (0.1940) are significantly more accurate than the default spaced+NNI pipeline (0.1990; p=3.9×10⁻³ and p=7.7×10⁻³). We note that Co-phylog was tested at k=19 with default half-context on DNA; a systematic scan across parameter space may reveal configurations where Co-phylog performs better, though its fundamental reliance on context-matching makes it intrinsically vulnerable to indels. These results position k-mer frequency vectors with cosine distance as among the most robust alignment-free approaches for gene-length phylogenetic inference across sequence types. Two method families remain untested — FFP [31], which pioneered feature frequency profiles for whole-genome comparison, and the broader spaced-word frequency family [13] (both were outside the scope of the current benchmark harness); systematic comparison under indel-rich conditions represents future work.

Future work will explore: (1) optimized k-mer sets and fusion weights for the multi-k ensemble, potentially including spaced k-mers in the ensemble and length-aware k selection; (2) validation on real gene-length DNA families with documented indel-rich evolution and trusted trees, extending the real-data validation beyond protein families, mitochondrial genomes and loci (low-indel), and bacterial genomes; (3) Mash benchmark across a range of indel rates (0.005–0.05) to quantify the MinHash collapse threshold (the n=30 benchmark at indel=0.02 is now complete, Table 2); (4) boundary classifier validation on real biological datasets with known phylogenetic structure; (5) integration as a rapid exploratory analysis module within existing phylogenetic pipelines; (6) application to metagenomic and single-cell datasets where alignment is particularly challenging; and (7) quartet-based DCM assembly to overcome the EPA grafting bottleneck at large scales.

### Practical recommendations

For practitioners, our results suggest the following guidelines:
- **Small-to-medium datasets (n≤1000) with expected indel rates above 0.01**: Consider Fusang with the multi-k ensemble (via `benchmark_multik_ensemble.py`) as a first-pass analysis. On the 30-seed L=1000 bp benchmark the ensemble is significantly less accurate than MAFFT+FastTree2 (nRF=0.137±0.023 vs 0.124±0.026, paired Wilcoxon p=0.0024), while requiring no alignment step and no manual k selection; in our harness the two are comparable in wall-clock time at this scale (median 44.5 s vs 39.0 s per seed), so the advantage is workflow simplicity rather than raw speed at n=200. Both remain distant from the true tree (nRF≈0.12–0.15), so outputs should be treated as rapid first-pass topologies. The ensemble provides the best accuracy among the alignment-free configurations tested at L=1000 bp (Table 5) and — together with contiguous k=7 — at L=500 bp as well (Table 4: 0.1940 vs 0.1990 for the default pipeline, p=0.0077), while avoiding manual k selection.
- **Clean substitution-only data (no indels)**: Mash (MinHash Jaccard, k=21) is far less accurate than k-mer cosine distances. The 30-seed benchmark (Table 2) shows Mash nRF=0.3486 ± 0.0351 vs Fusang nRF=0.2364 ± 0.0336 on clean data (Mash worse by +0.1122, paired Wilcoxon p=1.73×10⁻⁶, d=3.12, Fusang better in 30/30 seeds); the earlier figures (0.394 vs 0.376) came from the defective simulator and are withdrawn. K-mer cosine distances (Fusang) are the recommended alignment-free approach for both clean and indel-rich data, with the caveat that on clean data MSA+ML remains significantly more accurate than any alignment-free configuration we tested (Table 1).
- **Large datasets (n>1000)**: MSA-based methods remain preferred for accuracy; Fusang provides a valuable speed-accuracy trade-off for rapid exploratory analysis
- **Deep-divergence genome-scale data (organelle genomes, divergent gene families)**: The multi-k cosine ensemble reaches the community-benchmark ceiling on real mitochondrial genomes (Table 12; nRF=0.045, matched by MSA+ML and all top published methods) without alignment and without k selection; the gene-scale default k=5 saturates at genome length — use the multi-k ensemble (via `benchmark_multik_ensemble.py`) instead of any single small k
- **Shallow strain-level divergence (recombination-dominated)**: For within-species bacterial collections, anchor-based methods (andi, Co-phylog, kmacs) or Mash are better suited than k-mer cosine distances (Table 12)
- **Indel-rich data at any scale**: Fusang's alignment-free nature provides robustness that is not available from MSA-based methods, regardless of scale. Under indels, MinHash-based approaches are far less accurate than k-mer cosine (Mash 0.4034 ± 0.0844 vs Fusang 0.1926 ± 0.0272 at indel rate 0.02, 30 seeds; Fusang better in 30/30, d=2.61) and are the only family we tested that significantly degrades, so MinHash should be avoided for gene-length data; the earlier figure of nRF≈0.76 (~1.9× degradation) is withdrawn. At gene length (L≈500 bp), prefer contiguous k=7 or the multi-k ensemble (0.1938 / 0.1940) over the default spaced k=5 pipeline (0.1990) when maximum accuracy is wanted; the default remains a robust choice and is the configuration used throughout the timings and the large-scale benchmarks.
- **Computational constraints**: Fusang requires no alignment step and runs in seconds to minutes on a single CPU core, making it suitable for rapid iteration during exploratory analysis

**Supplementary Note S17.** 16S rRNA dataset annotation incident and remediation. The accession→organism mapping table (`CURATED_16S` in `download_16s_robust.py`) originally shipped with this dataset was erroneous at the genus/species/strain level for all 73 downloaded accessions. The sequences themselves are genuine GenBank 16S rRNA records — each was verified against NCBI as byte-identical (identity=1.0000 on spot checks) — so the error was in the labels, not the sequences. We re-annotated every accession against NCBI Taxonomy via its TaxID and recomputed every taxonomy-dependent analysis (Table 9 permutation tests, monophyly recovery, RF against the taxonomy-derived reference tree) under the corrected annotation; all values reported in the main text use this corrected annotation. The incident also surfaced a second, independent defect: the archived alignment (`real_16S_full_aligned.fasta`) was not genuinely aligned (cross-species pairwise identity fell below the 0.25 random baseline), so the alignment-based comparator was rebuilt end-to-end (MAFFT 7.526 `--localpair --maxiterate 1000` + FastTree2 `-nt -gtr -nosupport`). The full incident record, true dataset composition, and reproduction steps are provided in the reproducibility package (`real_data/real_16S_TRUE_label_incident.md`, `real_16S_monophyly.json`). The Fusang method itself is unaffected: it is alignment-free and never consumed the erroneous annotation. Earlier evaluations computed against the erroneous labels (including the rank-signal values of the archived analysis) are superseded.
